Geometric Time Score Estimators: Bridging Self-Predictive Representations and Generative Occupancy Models for Reinforcement Learning

Aravind Venugopal¹, Grace Liu¹, Jeff Schneider¹, Benjamin Eysenbach²
¹ Carnegie Mellon University  ·  ² Princeton University
📄 Paper 💻 Code
Can occupancy density estimation along probability paths bridge the gap between self-predictive representations and generative occupancy models?

The time score \(\partial_k \log p_k(g^+ \mid s, a)\) captures the instantaneous rate of change of the log-density of the occupancy measure along a probability path. Integrating it gives accurate occupancy density estimates for downstream control.

Overview

Predicting the consequences of actions by reasoning about the future is central to reinforcement learning (RL). While self-predictive representations have been a dominant paradigm for occupancy density estimation, we find that they yield surprisingly poor estimates. On the other hand, generative occupancy models can produce remarkably accurate samples from the occupancy measure. However, such models struggle with occupancy density estimation because they are not explicitly trained to estimate densities. This leaves their expressivity largely untapped for the temporal reasoning that downstream control requires. These complementary limitations raise the question: can occupancy density estimation along probability paths bridge the gap between self-predictive representations and generative occupancy models? In this paper, we explore this question.

The occupancy measure

Start an agent at state \(s\) and take action \(a\). The occupancy measure is the distribution over the states \(s^+\) it visits in the future:

\[ d^{\pi}(s^+ \mid s,a) = (1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t\, P(s_{t+1}=s^+ \mid s,a,\pi) \]

In goal-conditioned RL, \(d^{\pi}(g \mid s,a)\) equals the Q-value for reaching goal \(g\). Choosing the action that makes \(g\) most likely is choosing the best action, so a good density estimate is a good value estimate. The maze below follows a random-walk policy \(\pi\). How well do existing methods estimate occupancy density?
Poorly, even on a 5-step MDP. On a 5-step toy MDP in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with a branching, multimodal occupancy measure, the best baselines (CRL, TMD, TD-flows) reach a Spearman correlation coefficient of \(\rho = 0.78\) with the ground-truth \(\log d^{\pi_D}\). FB, TD-JEPA and Dual GR are near chance (0.19, 0.09, 0.19). CRL and TMD rank states near the origin well, where most of the mass is, but fail on far states.

Geometric Time Score Estimators
  1. Occupancy modeling along probability paths
    • The occupancy measure \(d^{\pi_D}(s^+ \mid s, a)\) is often a complex multimodal distribution. Instead of estimating it directly, we connect a simple base distribution \(p_0 = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) to \(p_1(\cdot \mid s,a) \equiv d^{\pi_D}(\cdot \mid s,a)\) with a variance-preserving probability path: \[ s_k^+ = \alpha_k s^+ + \sqrt{1-\alpha_k^2}\, s_0^+, \qquad k \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1] \]
    • The time score \(\partial_k \log p_k(s^+ \mid s,a)\) is the rate of change of log-density along the path. Integrating it recovers the log occupancy density: \[ \log d^{\pi_D}(s^+ \mid s,a) = \int_0^1 \partial_k \log p_k(s^+ \mid s,a)\, dk + \log p_0(s^+) \]
    • Conditioning on the endpoint \(s^+\) gives a closed-form regression target \(\partial_k \log p_k(s_k^+ \mid s^+)\) for a network \(s_\theta(s_k^+, k, s, a)\).

  2. Geometric Time Score Matching
    • GTSE trains a network \(s_\theta(s_k^+, k, s, a)\) to predict the time score at every \(k\) along the path. Monte Carlo targets cannot stitch trajectory segments, so we use the recursive structure of the occupancy measure to bootstrap time-score predictions from the next state-action pair:
    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{GTSE}}(\theta) = (1-\gamma)\, {\color{#d4883e}\mathcal{L}_{\text{next}}(\theta)} + \gamma\, {\color{#641e16}\mathcal{L}_{\text{future}}(\theta)}, \qquad \forall (s,a,s',a') \in \mathcal{D} \] \[ {\color{#d4883e}\mathcal{L}_{\text{next}}(\theta)} = \mathbb{E}_{k,\, s'_k \mid s'}\Big[\lambda(k)\big(\partial_k \log p_k(s'_k \mid s') - s_\theta(s'_k, k, s, a)\big)^2\Big] \] \[ {\color{#641e16}\mathcal{L}_{\text{future}}(\theta)} = \mathbb{E}_{s^+ \sim d^{\pi_D}(\cdot \mid s',a')}\, \mathbb{E}_{k,\, s^+_k \mid s^+}\Big[\lambda(k)\big(\operatorname{sg}[s_{\bar\theta}(s^+_k, k, s', a')] - s_\theta(s^+_k, k, s, a)\big)^2\Big] \]
    • Inference with \(N\) time-score evaluations: \(\log \hat{d}^{\pi_D}(s^+ \mid s,a) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} s_\theta(s^+, i/N, s, a) + \log p_0(s^+)\).
  3. Why it works: Multiscale future prediction
    • If we use a bilinear form for the time score, \(\phi(s,a,k)^\top \psi(g,k)\), the time-score integral has an exact Legendre decomposition: \[ \log d^{\pi_D}(g \mid s,a) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \hat{\phi}_l(s,a)^\top \hat{\psi}_l(g) + \log p_0(g) \]
    • Truncating at order \(L\) gives rank up to \((L+1)\delta\). Prior self-predictive methods learn rank-\(\delta\) approximations, which are analogous to \(L = 0\).
    • GTSE-b and GTSE-b\(_L\): We train GTSE with this bilinear form (GTSE-b) and, at inference, keep only the first \(L\) Legendre terms (GTSE-b\(_L\)). Comparing \(L = 0, 2, 10, \infty\) shows how much capacity the higher-order terms add.
  4. GTSE-\(r\): Explicit representations. Parameterizing \(s_\theta(g,k,s,a) = \phi_\theta(s,a)^\top \psi_\theta(g,k)\) gives \(\log \hat{d} \propto \phi_\theta(s,a)^\top \bar{\psi}_\theta(g)\): a state-action representation and a goal representation learned along probability paths.
Empirical Analysis
Q1.Is GTSE a better occupancy estimator, and does that matter for control?
Yes. GTSE achieves \(\rho = 0.96\) on the toy MDP and stays on the diagonal for far states (select GTSE above). On pointmaze-large-diverse, baselines degrade on far goals (\(\rho \le 0.30\)), and the best one (CRL) succeeds 3% of the time with best-of-16 action selection. GTSE ranks actions best for both near (\(\rho = 0.63\)) and far (\(\rho = 0.55\)) goals and succeeds 38% of the time.

Q2.Where does GTSE's representational capacity come from?
From occupancy modeling along probability paths. We use the truncated Legendre decomposition to empirically analyse the effect of representational capacity. On the toy MDP, \(\rho\) rises from 0.82 at \(L=0\) to 0.96 at \(L=\infty\) (no truncation). On pointmaze, success rises from 12% to 25%. Even \(L = 0\) beats every baseline, so training along probability paths helps on its own. Surprisingly, GTSE-r, which learns explicit state-action and goal representations, also performs well: \(\rho = 0.95\) on the toy MDP and 25 ± 2% success on pointmaze.

Q3.Do GTSE and GTSE-r improve downstream policy performance on challenging long-horizon goal-conditioned RL tasks?
Yes, by 2× to 6×. Across 7 OGBench datasets, GTSE averages 37.9% success: 2× the strongest baseline (TD-JEPA, 18.6%) and 6× the weakest (TMD, 6.4%). GTSE-r is second (31.6%). GTSE is best on every manipulation dataset, including cube-triple. On antmaze, GTSE-r is ahead (78% vs 53%), likely because goal density there depends almost only on the agent's (x, y) position. All methods use best-of-16 action selection with the same flow-matching BC policy, so only the estimator differs.

Q4.Can the representations learned by GTSE-r effectively transfer across reward-based RL tasks?

scene-play

Yes. We freeze each method's representation encoder and train a Flow Q-Learning (FQL) critic on top of it, on the 5 reward-based scene-play tasks. Frozen GTSE-r features get the highest mean success, 62.2%, against 46.6% for TD-JEPA and 58.2% for vanilla FQL trained end-to-end. GTSE-r matches FQL on tasks 1 to 3, where both are near saturation. On task 4 it scores about 4× higher (36% vs 9%).
